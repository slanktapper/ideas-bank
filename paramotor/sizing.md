# Sizing — the numbers everything else follows from

Captured 2026-09-07 MDT.

## The pilot

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Height | 6'8" = 203 cm |
| Weight | 285 lb = 129.3 kg |

Both numbers matter, and they bind differently. Weight drives wing, thrust and
reserve. Height drives harness geometry and frame fit. They are separate problems
that happen to land on the same person.

## All-up weight (AUW)

Every certification figure in PPG is written against *total* weight in flight —
pilot, clothing, motor, fuel, reserve, everything. Nothing is certified against
naked pilot weight, which is why "the paramotor is rated to 160 kg" is such a
misleading thing to read.

| Item | Low | High |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pilot | 129.3 kg | 129.3 kg |
| Helmet, boots, suit, gloves, radio | 2.5 kg | 3.5 kg |
| Paramotor, dry (big-engine unit) | 26 kg | 32 kg |
| Fuel (10 L / 15 L at ~0.74 kg/L) | 7.4 kg | 11.1 kg |
| Reserve parachute (heavy-rated) | 2.0 kg | 2.8 kg |
| **All-up weight** | **167 kg** | **179 kg** |

**Working number: ~170 kg (375 lb)** with a modest tank. Call it 175 kg for
planning, because tanks get filled and jackets get worn.

This single number is the whole story. Hold onto it.

## What 170 kg gates

### 1. The wing — the tightest constraint

Wing loading is AUW divided by flat area. A beginner-to-intermediate PPG pilot
wants roughly 4.0–5.0 kg/m²; above about 5.5 kg/m² the wing gets fast and
unforgiving, which is exactly wrong for a student.

| Flat area | Wing loading at 170 kg |
| --- | --- |
| 24 m² | 7.1 kg/m² — no |
| 27 m² | 6.3 kg/m² — no |
| 29 m² | 5.9 kg/m² — sporty, not for training |
| 31 m² | 5.5 kg/m² — the floor |
| 34 m² | 5.0 kg/m² — the target |

So the wing needs to be **31–34 m² flat**. That is the largest size in most
manufacturers' solo ranges, and in several ranges it does not exist at all.

The second problem is certification. Most EN-certified solo PPG wings top out
around 130–150 kg total weight; the very largest sizes reach about 170 kg. As a
worked example, the Dudek Universal 1.1 in size 34 carries an **EN range of
135–170 kg and a DGAC (ultralight) range of 135–215 kg** — and Dudek notes that
size 34 ships without a speed system because it is intended for trikes.

That is the shape of the whole market: at 170 kg you sit *at the very top* of the
EN range on the biggest solo wings, and comfortably inside the DGAC/ULM ranges that
sit above them. Wings at the top of their certified range are less forgiving, sink
faster, land faster, and have less margin. Flying at the ceiling of a wing's range
is legal and common; it is not the same as flying in the middle of it.

Practical shortlist consequence: you are shopping the **top size only**, and often
the top size is trike-oriented or DGAC-only. See `gear.md`.

### 2. Thrust — Alberta makes this worse

Rule of thumb for comfortable foot launch: static thrust of roughly 55–65% of AUW.
At 170 kg that is **95–110 kg of thrust at sea level**.

Then subtract Alberta. Central Alberta strips run roughly 2,900–3,600 ft field
elevation, and on a warm summer afternoon density altitude at those fields
comfortably exceeds 6,000 ft. Normally-aspirated engines and props both lose
performance with density — the working rule is about **3% of power per 1,000 ft of
density altitude**, so DA 6,500 ft costs you somewhere near 19%. Ground roll
degrades faster still: general aviation guidance puts takeoff roll up 25% or more
per 1,000 ft of DA.

Which means: **spec 110–120 kg of sea-level static thrust** so that ~90–95 kg is
still there on a July afternoon. A motor that looks adequate on paper at sea level
is not adequate here.

The corollary is a flying-habit one, not a gear one: morning and evening. The
difference between a 07:00 and a 14:00 launch at a high-elevation field is commonly
2,000–3,000 ft of density altitude, and at your weight that is the difference
between a comfortable launch and a marginal one.

### 3. The reserve — the one people miss

Reserve parachutes are certified (EN 12491) against **suspended weight**, which for
you is the full ~170 kg, not your body weight.

Standard solo reserves are certified to roughly 100–120 kg. The heavier solo
options reach 140 kg — the APCO Mayday Square 140, for instance, is rated 80–140 kg
and quotes a descent rate under 5.5 m/s *at* 140 kg.

**170 kg is over all of them.** You need a tandem-class or heavy-rated reserve
(typically 180–220 kg). This is not optional and it is not a place to save money:
an overloaded reserve descends faster than its certified rate, and the whole point
of the reserve is the one flight where that matters.

### 4. The frame — where "no" gets said out loud

This is the constraint that surprises people, because manufacturers publish a
*pilot* weight limit separate from any total-weight figure.

The Parajet Maverick — a mainstream, well-regarded frame, including the MAX variant
marketed for "experienced, tandem and large pilots" — publishes a **maximum pilot
weight of 265 lb (120 kg)**.

You are 285 lb. You are over that frame's published limit by about 9 kg, on a model
specifically sold as the big-pilot option in its range. Many other manufacturers
publish the same 120 kg figure.

So the frame search is not "which frame is nicest", it is **"who publishes a number
above 129 kg, and who will build to spec"** — and it needs asking in writing, per
model, because a dealer's verbal "you'll be fine" is worth nothing when a
weld fails.

## The height problem, separately

203 cm is above where the harness market stops advertising. APCO's XL harness sizing
guidance, as a representative data point, covers **pilots up to 2 m tall and up to
120 kg** — you are over on both axes simultaneously, which is the crux: it is not
that you are tall, and it is not that you are heavy, it is that the XL option is
sized for one or the other and you need both.

What actually goes wrong when the harness is too small for the pilot:

- **Hang point geometry.** Hang points set where the motor sits relative to your
  shoulders and where the thrust line points. Get it wrong and the machine rides
  badly, the thrust vector fights you, and weight-shift steering stops working the
  way it should.
- **Torso length vs. back plate.** A 203 cm frame on a back plate built for 185 cm
  puts the motor low on your back, which drops the prop arc closer to the ground on
  the launch run — exactly when you are leaning forward and least able to fix it.
- **Leg strap and seat geometry.** Long femurs change where the seat catches you.
  A seat that fits badly is uncomfortable for the first hour and a control problem
  after that.
- **Leverage.** A tall pilot is a longer lever. The same 35 kg machine on your back
  wobbles more during the run than it does on someone 20 cm shorter.

Good news on one point: long legs mean *more* ground clearance under the cage
standing still, not less. The clearance problem is dynamic — the forward lean during
the run — not static.

The practical answer here is almost certainly a **custom or made-to-measure
harness**, ordered against actual body measurements, not an off-the-shelf XL. Several
manufacturers do this. It adds cost and lead time.

## Foot launch vs. wheels

Worth stating plainly because it changes every constraint above.

**Foot launching at your size means running — accelerating, in a forward lean, on
uneven grass — with 170 kg of total system, of which ~40 kg is strapped to your
back.** That load goes through your knees and ankles on every launch and every
landing. The consensus in the sport is straightforward: heavier pilots take
substantially more joint stress on foot launch, and a wheeled machine is the
better choice for anyone carrying that load or already dealing with knees or back.

Trikes and quads are commonly rated to **350–400 lb and up**, which makes the frame
pilot-weight problem disappear. The wing problem does not disappear — you still need
the big wing — but the big wings are often *designed* for trikes anyway, which
resolves the "size 34 has no speed system because it's for trikes" awkwardness in
your favour.

The trade: wheels are heavier, more expensive, need a decent launch and landing
surface, are harder to transport, and give up the pack-it-in-the-car spontaneity
that draws a lot of people to foot launch in the first place. Wheeled flight is also
generally slower for a given wing.

Not decided. But at 6'8" and 285 lb, a trike is a legitimate *first* option rather
than a fallback, and it should be evaluated as such rather than after a foot-launch
attempt goes badly.

## Verdict

**Viable, but not from a catalogue.** Nothing here is a physics wall — 170 kg AUW is
inside what certified equipment can carry. What it is, is a narrow corridor: top-size
wing, biggest-class engine, tandem-rated reserve, a frame whose maker publishes a
number above 129 kg, and a harness built to measure. Every one of those is a special
order rather than a stock item, which means lead time and a dealer who will put the
figures in writing.

The single highest-leverage variable is weight, because it moves all four constraints
at once. At around 120 kg pilot weight the frame ratings stop excluding you, the
standard XL harnesses come into range, 140 kg-class reserves become adequate, and
the wing sits mid-range instead of at its ceiling. Recording that as arithmetic, not
as advice.
